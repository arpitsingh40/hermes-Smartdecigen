# V82 · THE LITTLE BOOK THAT BEATS THE MARKET — Joel Greenblatt
Tier 2 · Investing · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
Buying good companies at bargain prices — companies earning high returns on capital when the market has temporarily priced them as if they don't — consistently outperforms the market over time; the method is simple enough for a child, but the discipline to STICK with it is what separates investors.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Greenblatt's Magic Formula: rank all companies by (1) earnings yield (how cheap is the business relative to its earnings?) and (2) return on capital (how good is the business at turning investment into profit?), then buy the top combined rank in equal weights, rebalance annually. The formula works because the market is emotionally-driven in the short term — it throws out high-quality companies with bad quarters alongside genuinely bad businesses. A founder's version: find the businesses or moves in YOUR portfolio where the intrinsic quality is high (strong power, good unit economics) but the market has temporarily underpriced them (low multiple, forgotten, out of favor). The same pattern — buy good at a bad price — applies to acquiring competitors, hiring overlooked talent, and investing in capabilities when no one else is.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — Earnings yield = value
├─ Earnings yield is the inverse of P/E: how much earnings you get per rupee invested; it measures CHEAPNESS
├─ Mr. Market (Benjamin Graham's metaphor) periodically offers great businesses at awful prices; the Magic Formula systematically scans for those moments
└─ LEAF: for any investment (tool, hire, channel, acquisition), compute the earnings-yield equivalent: what return does this produce relative to cost? If the yield is high because others are avoiding it, interest intensifies

### Branch 2 — Return on capital = quality
├─ ROC = how efficiently a business turns investment into profit; High ROC → competitive advantage (moat)
├─ The magic: combining CHEAP (high earnings yield) with QUALITY (high ROC) filters out value traps — cheap businesses that deserve to be cheap
└─ LEAF: audit each business unit / product line for ROC; the ones with high ROC AND temporarily suppressed earnings are the ones to double-down on

### Branch 3 — The rebalancing discipline
├─ The formula works only if you REBALANCE annually — selling the ones that have gone up, buying the ones that have fallen back into the zone
├─ This is emotionally hard: winners feel good, losers feel bad — the formula buys what feels bad at rebalance time
└─ LEAF: apply rebalancing to the founder's initiative portfolio — which initiatives have run up past their justified weight? Which are undervalued? Rebalance annually

### Branch 4 — Mean reversion is a tailwind
├─ The market overreacts to bad news; earnings recover more often than companies die; the formula works because mean reversion is a statistical fact
├─ Exception: companies with deteriorating ROC (the moat is gone) — the formula catches these by requiring high ROC
└─ LEAF: mean reversion works ONCE — assess whether the ROC deterioration is structural or cyclical before acting

### Branch 5 — Simple beats complex
├─ Greenblatt's point: a 5th grader can run the formula; professional investors dismiss it as too simple — and underperform because of it
├─ Complexity is usually ego, not accuracy; simple rules applied with discipline outperform sophisticated models applied with emotion
└─ LEAF: when a founder's analysis becomes complex, ask "can this be simplified to one decision rule?" — if the formula gets too complicated, execution suffers

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN evaluating a potential acquisition → APPLY Magic Formula lens: what is its earnings yield and ROC? Buying without both numbers is guessing.
- WHEN deciding which initiative to fund → APPLY portfolio rebalancing: rank initiatives by quality (ROC-like measure) × cheapness (cost relative to impact); fund the top, cut the bottom.
- WHEN a founder falls in love with a "great business" at any price → APPLY earnings-yield discipline: what return does the price imply? If it's < safe alternatives, it's a bad investment regardless of quality.
- WHEN the market environment is fearful → APPLY contrarian quality scan: which high-ROC assets are temporarily mispriced by fear?
- WHEN the founder's initiative list has grown without pruning → APPLY annual rebalancing: sell the past year's winners (redeploy to new undervalued bets), cut the persistent losers.
