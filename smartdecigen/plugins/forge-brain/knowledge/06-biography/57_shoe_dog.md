# V57 · SHOE DOG — Phil Knight
Tier 4 · Biography · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
Building a great company is not a rational process — it is a CRAZY, MESSY, LONELY DECADE of survival where every day something goes wrong, the odds are overwhelmingly against you, and the only thing that keeps going is an irrational stubbornness — a "crazy idea" you refuse to abandon even when every fact says you should.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Phil Knight's memoir of building Nike from a one-man import operation to the world's most iconic brand. The core: Knight was a mediocre runner and a diffident accountant who, on a trip around the world, decided to import Japanese running shoes to America. The company nearly died a dozen times — cash crises, supplier betrayals, legal fights with the government, banks that cut him off, partners who tried to steal the company, the betrayal of his first employee (and best friend), and the constant threat of bankruptcy. The book is about PERSISTENCE: not strategy, not vision, not brilliant moves — just refusal to quit. Key characters (Bowerman, Johnson, Strasser, Woodell, Hayes) each contributed different genius. The Japanese partnership (Onitsuka Tiger) was both the lifeline and the trap. The breakthrough was not a product — it was the WAFFLE trainer (Bowerman's waffle iron sole) and the brand soul that Knight accidentally built by marketing to the rebellious athlete.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The crazy idea
├─ Knight's "crazy idea": sell Japanese running shoes in America — no business plan, no market research, just a feeling
├─ The entrepreneurial starting point is always an idea that sounds irrational to everyone else
├─ Knight's father: "What's your backup plan?" Knight: "I don't have one." — the honest answer
└─ LEAF: when a founder's idea looks irrational, that's not automatically wrong — Knight's was insane on paper; the question is not "is it rational?" but "are you committed to finding out?"

### Branch 2 — The cash crisis as permanent state
├─ Nike was ALWAYS out of money for the first decade — banks called loans, suppliers demanded cash, payroll was a weekly prayer
├─ Knight's solution: partner with Nissho Iwai (a Japanese trading company) that extended credit when US banks wouldn't
├─ The "bank of Mom" was real: Knight lived on savings, spaghetti, and never knew if next month's bills could be paid
└─ LEAF: when a founder panics about cash flow, normalize it — Knight survived 10+ years on the edge; cash crises are the DEFAULT state of a growing company, not a bug; the skill is not avoiding them but surviving them

### Branch 3 — The people who made Nike
├─ Bill Bowerman: the obsessive coach-inventor who created performance shoes in his garage with a waffle iron — the product genius
├─ Jeff Johnson: the eccentric first employee who understood the CUSTOMER — wrote love letters from customers to the company, built the brand culture from nothing
├─ Bob Woodell: the disabled former athlete turned operational backbone — built the supply chain
├─ John Jaqua: the lawyer who saved the company in the FTC/Onitsuka legal battle
└─ LEAF: Knight didn't hire for resumes — he hired people who were OBSESSED (even weirdly obsessed) with running, shoes, or the mission; when hiring, look for obsessive domain interest over polished credentials

### Branch 4 — The partnership trap and the break
├─ Onitsuka Tiger (the Japanese supplier) was Nike's only source — they had all the power
├─ When Onitsuka tried to buy Knight's company or cut him out, Knight had to start his OWN manufacturing (a totally new, terrifying capability)
├─ The lesson: every single-supplier relationship is a ticking time bomb; the break is brutal but inevitable
└─ LEAF: when a founder relies on a single supplier, distribution partner, or platform, flag the Onitsuka risk: they WILL eventually compete with you or control your margin — build redundancy before you need it

### Branch 5 — The brand soul: marketing to the rebel
├─ Nike's break: positioning not as a shoe company but as the voice of the ATHLETE against the establishment
├─ "Just Do It" captured the ethos: Nike was for the competitor who refused to accept limits
├─ This connected with runners who felt ignored by the sneaker establishment (Adidas, Puma)
├─ The brand became a MOVEMENT, not a product — and movements survive product failures
└─ LEAF: when a founder asks about brand, tell them Knight's lesson: brand is not a logo or tagline — it's the CAUSE you stand for that your customers believe in too; Nike stood for the underdog athlete; what does your company stand for?

### Branch 6 — The IPO and the lesson about money
├─ After 15+ years, Nike went public at $22/share (now split-adjusted ~$50,000+)
├─ Knight could have taken money earlier — many offers — but believed the company was worth more and maintained control
├─ The final message: wealth is not the goal — the JOURNEY of building with people you love and believe in is the real reward
└─ LEAF: when a founder fixates on exit valuation, tell them Knight's perspective: the value of the journey (the struggle with people you respect, the obstacles overcome) is worth more than the exit check; a premature exit trades the story for money

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder feels the cash crisis is abnormal → APPLY Shoe Dog normalization: Knight survived 10+ years on the verge of bankruptcy; cash pressure is not a sign of failure, it's the default state.
- WHEN a founder relies on one supplier/distributor → APPLY Onitsuka warning: build redundancy before the partner has leverage to squeeze or compete.
- WHEN a founder is uncertain about hiring → APPLY obsession test: do they care DEEPLY about the domain, not just the job? Hire the obsessed.
- WHEN the founder needs brand strategy → APPLY movement positioning: what cause does your company stand for? What do your customers believe that the establishment doesn't understand?
- WHEN a founder considers an early exit → APPLY journey reframe: look at Knight's 15-year arc before the IPO; the value is in the building, not the exit.
