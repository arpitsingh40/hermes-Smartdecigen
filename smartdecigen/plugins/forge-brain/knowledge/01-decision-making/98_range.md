# V98 · RANGE — David Epstein
Tier 2 · Learning & Development · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
In complex, ill-structured domains (where the rules are not fixed and the problems are novel), generalists — people with broad, varied experience — consistently outperform specialists; the path to excellence is NOT early specialization and narrow practice, but sampling widely, making connections across fields, and integrating diverse knowledge.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Epstein dismantles the "10,000-hour rule" (deliberate practice from early specialization) as the only path to excellence. That rule applies in KIND domains (chess, golf, violin) where the rules are stable and feedback is immediate and clear. In WICKED domains (entrepreneurship, investing, innovation) — where rules change, problems are novel, and feedback is delayed — the key is RANGE: sampling different fields, collecting diverse experiences, making analogic connections across domains, and developing conceptual, integrative thinking over procedural skill. The most innovative teams and individuals are not those with the deepest expertise in one area but those who can BRIDGE knowledge across areas. For founders, the implication is profound: the early-over-specialization advice is wrong for entrepreneurship. The best founders are often late bloomers with diverse backgrounds who can connect dots across markets, technologies, and business models. The engine should celebrate breadth, encourage exploration of adjacent domains, and frame founder "distractions" (curiosity about other fields) as potential innovation sources.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — Kind vs Wicked domains
├─ Kind domain: rules are clear, patterns repeat, feedback is fast and accurate — chess, golf, firefighting (after thousands of fires)
├─ Wicked domain: rules change, patterns may not repeat, feedback is delayed or ambiguous — startup strategy, investing, innovation, management
├─ Deliberate practice (10,000 hours) works in KIND domains; RANGE is the advantage in WICKED domains
├─ LEAF: classify the founder's core challenges — are they kind (hiring, accounting, legal compliance) or wicked (product strategy, market timing, competitive response)? Treat them differently
└─ LEAF: a founder with 10,000 hours of narrow expertise in a wicked domain may be LESS effective than a generalist with 5 years of broad experience — because the narrow expert has learned the wrong patterns

### Branch 2 — The learning curve: sampling before specializing
├─ Most innovators and successful founders sampled broadly in their 20s before finding their niche in their 30s and 40s
├─ The "head start" of early specialization is a mirage — quitters and samplers catch up because their broad base gives them creative insight
├─ LEAF: when a founder worries about not having enough domain experience, reframe: your breadth is an asset in proportion to how wicked the problem is
└─ LEAF: encourage founders to spend time in ADJACENT domains — if you run a D2C brand, spend a day with a supply chain expert, a day with a politician, a day with an artist

### Branch 3 — Analogical thinking: connecting across domains
├─ The most powerful innovation tool: taking a solution from one domain and applying it to a problem in another (e.g., applying evolutionary biology to product strategy, or military doctrine to go-to-market)
├─ The drawback: experts often suffer from "Einstellung" — the tendency to keep using the same solution even when better ones exist
├─ LEAF: when a founder is stuck, ask: "where in nature, in history, in a completely different industry does this problem exist and what solutions did they find?"
└─ LEAF: the best founder superpower: the ability to translate insights from X (psychology, physics, gaming, warfare) into the startup domain

### Branch 4 — The late bloomer advantage
├─ Early bloomers dominate in kind domains (chess prodigies, math olympians); late bloomers dominate in wicked ones (entrepreneurs, innovators, writers)
├─ Van Gogh sold one painting in his lifetime; Julia Child published her first cookbook at 50; most Nobel-winning scientists had their breakthrough later than the "peak" age
├─ LEAF: founder age is not a liability — later-life founders bring pattern recognition, network breadth, and a sense of what actually matters
└─ LEAF: for a young founder, the best advice is NOT to double-down on one domain — travel, try different roles, explore; the range will pay off

### Branch 5 — Team range: why diverse teams win
├─ The best teams for wicked problems are not all-star specialists from one domain — they are teams with diversity of background, training, and thinking style
├─ Homogeneous expert teams produce elegant but brittle solutions; diverse teams produce messier but more robust solutions
├─ LEAF: when building a founding team, prioritize cognitive diversity (different backgrounds, industries, thinking styles) over shared domain expertise
└─ LEAF: the most common founder mistake in hiring: cloning themselves. Hire people whose weaknesses are your strengths and whose strengths are your weaknesses

### Branch 6 — The test: breadth before depth
├─ Epstein's prescription: try many things; when you find the thing that grips you, go deep — but don't go deep before you've sampled
├─ The "match quality" theory: you can't know what you're good at and passionate about without trying it; sampling is the only way to discover fit
└─ LEAF: when evaluating a founder's career history, don't penalize "lack of focus" — a diverse background is often a RANGE asset, not a liability

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder doubts their broad background → APPLY range reframe: "in wicked domains like startups, breadth is an asset — your diverse experience gives you analogies that narrow experts lack."
- WHEN a founder is stuck on a problem → APPLY analogic thinking: "what's a completely different domain where a similar problem was solved? Steal that solution."
- WHEN a founder is building a team → APPLY diversity test: "is your founding team a clone circle or a set of complementary strengths from different domains?"
- WHEN a founder is pressured to "find their niche" early → APPLY sampling advice: "try 3-5 things in your 20s; commit to the one that grips you in your 30s."
- WHEN the founder is older and feels behind → APPLY late-bloomer framing: "your range of experiences is an unmatched asset for wicked problems; youthful focus is overrated here."
