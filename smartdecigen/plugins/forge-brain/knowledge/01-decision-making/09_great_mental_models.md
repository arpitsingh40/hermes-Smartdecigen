# V09 · THE GREAT MENTAL MODELS — Shane Parrish
Tier 2 · Mental Models · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The quality of your decisions is bounded by the accuracy of your map of reality — and since every map is a simplification, mastery lies in owning a small set of time-tested general models, knowing each one's limits, and choosing the right model for the terrain instead of forcing the terrain into your favorite model.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Parrish curates the nine foundational general-purpose models into a usable library. The meta-skill is MODEL SELECTION: most bad decisions aren't calculation errors, they're using the wrong lens entirely (optimizing a flywheel that should be questioned from first principles; psychoanalyzing a competitor whose behavior is simple incompetence). For a decision engine, this book is the routing table — which reasoning tool fires for which class of founder problem.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The map is not the territory
├─ All models (financial projections, personas, dashboards) are reductions; useful, never true
├─ Maps age: last year's ICP may describe a customer that no longer exists
└─ LEAF: when reality and the founder's spreadsheet disagree, reality wins — update the map, don't argue with the territory

### Branch 2 — Circle of competence
├─ Inside the circle: intuition has data behind it. Outside: intuition is impersonating expertise
├─ The circle's edge is discoverable through honest "how do I know this?" interrogation
└─ LEAF: route decisions outside the circle to base rates, experiments, and borrowed expertise

### Branch 3 — First-principles thinking
├─ Decompose to physics-level facts ("what do customers actually pay for?") and rebuild without inherited assumptions
├─ Expensive; reserve for stuck problems and industry-standard assumptions ("distributors always take 40%+" — says who? built on what cost structure?)
└─ LEAF: when a founder repeats an industry norm as a law, decompose it: which part is physics, which part is habit?

### Branch 4 — Thought experiment & inversion
├─ Simulate cheaply: "if you had 10x the budget, would you still choose this channel?" isolates conviction from constraint
├─ Inversion: solve backward from failure or from the desired end-state
└─ LEAF: use paired thought experiments to expose real preferences ("if both options paid the same, which would you pick?")

### Branch 5 — Second-order thinking
├─ First-order: the discount lifts sales. Second-order: customers learn to wait for discounts, margin structure erodes, brand repositions itself downmarket
├─ Almost everyone stops at first order; the edge lives at second and third
└─ LEAF: mandatory "and then what?" pass on every recommended action, one level deeper than comfortable

### Branch 6 — Probabilistic thinking
├─ Think in distributions, not outcomes; update Bayesian-style as evidence arrives
├─ Fat tails change everything: in fat-tailed domains the average is a lie (see Black Swan)
└─ LEAF: express engine judgments as calibrated probabilities with explicit priors

### Branch 7 — Occam's & Hanlon's razors
├─ Occam: prefer the explanation with fewest moving assumptions — sales dropped? Check the checkout bug before theorizing a market shift
├─ Hanlon: never attribute to malice what incompetence explains — the distributor who missed a call is probably disorganized, not scheming
└─ LEAF: rank explanations by assumption-count before acting on the dramatic one

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN diagnosing a surprise (sales drop, churn spike) → APPLY Occam+Hanlon ordering: simplest mechanical causes first, malice last.
- WHEN a founder treats an industry norm as immutable → APPLY first principles: decompose the norm into its cost-structure atoms and rebuild.
- WHEN recommending any action → APPLY second-order pass: name the reaction of customers, competitors, and the founder's own habits.
- WHEN the founder faces a class of problem → APPLY model routing: explicitly pick the lens (systems? probability? incentive? first principles?) and say why this lens fits this terrain.
- WHEN conviction is unclear → APPLY thought experiments to separate what the founder wants from what their constraints force.
