# V97 · SUPER THINKING — Gabriel Weinberg & Lauren McCann
Tier 2 · Mental Models · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The world is too complex to understand directly — so the best decision-makers use a lattice of mental models (simple, reusable frameworks from different disciplines) to see the underlying structure of problems and avoid being trapped by a single perspective.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Weinberg (DuckDuckGo founder) and McCann compile and systematize the most useful mental models across physics, biology, psychology, economics, and strategy. Their core point: mental models are not academic curiosities — they are PRACTICAL thinking tools that, when combined, let you see what others miss. A mental model is a simplified representation of a complex reality — a map of a territory that is useful even though it's not complete. The key skill is building a LATTICE of models — having enough different lenses that you can view any problem through multiple frames. Each model has a USE CASE and a TRAP (when it misleads). For founders, this book is a reference library: when stuck on a problem, scan the models — one of them will unlock the solution. The engine's job: map the problem to the right mental model and prevent the founder from applying a model in a context where it misleads.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The latticework: why you need many models
├─ "To a man with a hammer, everything looks like a nail" — a single model is dangerously over-applied
├─ A lattice of models gives multiple lenses: economic (incentives, supply/demand), psychological (biases, motivators), strategic (game theory, power), biological (evolution, competition)
├─ LEAF: when a founder has a single explanation for everything (e.g., "it's all about marketing" or "it's all about the product"), they need more models
└─ LEAF: the lattice is built over a career, not a weekend; add one model at a time, apply it until it becomes instinctive

### Branch 2 — Key models from the book (a selection)
├─ Occam's Razor: simplest explanation is most likely — don't multiply entities beyond necessity
├─ Hanlon's Razor: don't attribute to malice what can be explained by incompetence (or, even more useful: by incentives)
├─ Parkinson's Law: work expands to fill the time available — use hard deadlines to force efficient output
├─ Hofstadter's Law: everything takes longer than you expect, even when you account for Hofstadter's Law
├─ Confirmation Bias: the tendency to seek evidence that confirms existing beliefs — counter with disconfirmation-seeking
├─ Inversion: instead of asking "how do I achieve X?" ask "how could I cause X to fail?" then avoid those paths
├─ LEAF: for any founder problem, run the inversion model: "imagine we failed completely — what went wrong?" (Kahneman's premortem)
└─ LEAF: Parkinson's Law is a productivity lever — give the team half the time they request and watch them meet it

### Branch 3 — Thinking in trade-offs: the opportunity cost model
├─ Every yes is implicitly a no to something else; explicit trade-off analysis prevents spreading too thin
├─ The model: list the top 3 things you could do; for each, estimate the expected value and the opportunity cost (the best alternative foregone)
├─ LEAF: when a founder can't decide between two options, make the opportunity cost explicit: "if you choose A, what's the expected value of B that you're giving up?"
└─ LEAF: the hardest trade-off is between two good options — the model forces clarity

### Branch 4 — Feedback loops and systems thinking
├─ Balancing (stabilizing) loops: thermostats, competition that caps prices, customer complaints that improve quality
├─ Reinforcing (amplifying) loops: network effects, viral growth, funding → PR → more funding → more PR
├─ LEAF: identify the dominant loop in the founder's business — is it a virtuous cycle (reinforcing, positive) or a vicious cycle (reinforcing, negative)?
└─ LEAF: many founder problems come from intervening in a balancing loop without understanding it — the system pushes back

### Branch 5 — Probabilistic thinking
├─ The world is not deterministic; outcomes are probability distributions
├─ Expected value = probability × payoff; compare expected values, not best-case scenarios
├─ LEAF: when a founder presents a single "likely" number, ask for the full distribution: "worst case, best case, and what's the probability-weighted expected value?"
└─ LEAF: low-probability, high-payoff opportunities (venture bets) require different thinking than high-probability, low-payoff opportunities (operational bets)

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder is stuck on a problem → APPLY model scan: "what models apply here? Inversion? Hanlon's Razor? Opportunity cost? Feedback loops?"
- WHEN a founder has a single explanation → APPLY lattice expansion: "what would this look like through a different model — psychological, economic, biological?"
- WHEN a founder can't choose between options → APPLY opportunity-cost model: "what's the expected value of each, and what's the best alternative you're giving up?"
- WHEN a founder struggles with timelines → APPLY Parkinson + Hofstadter: "cut the timeline in half and add Hofstadter's Law — that's your real schedule."
- WHEN the founder's company is stuck in a pattern → APPLY loop diagnosis: "is this a reinforcing or balancing loop? What one change could flip it?"
