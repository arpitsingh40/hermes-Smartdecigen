# V72 · HOOKED — Nir Eyal
Tier 5 · Product Innovation · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The most successful products aren't the best designed — they are the ones that create a habit by cycling users through a four-step loop so effectively that the behavior becomes automatic, triggered by internal cues rather than external marketing.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Eyal's Hook Model explains why some products command daily attention while others languish: they build a HABIT through a four-phase cycle — TRIGGER (external: a notification, an ad; or internal: boredom, loneliness, anxiety) → ACTION (the simplest behavior in anticipation of a reward — must be easy to do) → VARIABLE REWARD (the craving is for the VARIABLE, not the reward itself — unpredictability drives dopamine) → INVESTMENT (the user puts something in — data, effort, reputation, followers — which makes the product more valuable and increases the likelihood of returning). The key insight: habits form when the trigger-action-reward-investment loop is repeated frequently enough that the internal trigger (the feeling) automatically cues the behavior. For founders, the model is a design tool: which internal trigger does your product soothe, and how quick and variable is the satisfaction?

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — Trigger: the cue that starts the loop
├─ Two types: external triggers (push notifications, emails, app icons) and internal triggers (emotions, situations, routines)
├─ The goal is to move from external to internal — from "the app notifies you" to "you feel bored and open the app"
├─ Internal triggers are the moat: once boredom = checking your app, competitors need to break the association, not just match features
└─ LEAF: for any product, identify the PRIMARY internal trigger (what emotional state does it soothe?) — if there isn't one, the product is not habit-forming

### Branch 2 — Action: the simplest behavior in anticipation of reward
├─ Action = motivation × ability × trigger (Fogg Behavior Model) — all three must converge
├─ The key lever is ABILITY: make the behavior as easy as possible (remove time, money, physical effort, social deviance, and cognitive load)
├─ Don't increase motivation — reduce friction. The product that is easiest to use wins the habit
└─ LEAF: audit the first three steps of the user's journey: remove one point of friction per step. The simplest action pattern wins retention

### Branch 3 — Variable reward: the craving engine
├─ The brain's dopamine system responds to the ANTICIPATION of reward, especially when the reward is unpredictable
├─ Three types: reward of the TRIBE (social validation, approval), reward of the HUNT (resources, information, money), reward of the SELF (mastery, competence, completion)
├─ Variable rewards must satisfy the internal trigger without misleading or manipulating beyond value — ethical hooks serve the user's need
└─ LEAF: map the product's reward type — if it offers the same reward every time (predictable), it will not form a habit; add variability

### Branch 4 — Investment: the commitment that compounds
├─ The user puts something in (data, content, followers, reputation) that makes the product better with each use
├─ Investment increases the likelihood of returning: the user has skin in the game
├─ Unlike a reward (which is received), an investment is given — and that giving creates ownership
└─ LEAF: identify what users invest IN the product — if they don't invest anything (no stored data, no created content, no social capital), retention depends entirely on novelty

### Branch 5 — Ethical considerations (the manipulative line)
├─ The Hook model is a tool — it can be used to form healthy habits (exercise, learning, saving) or unhealthy addictions
├─ Eyal's test: would the founder use the product themselves? Does it improve the user's life?
├─ The hook should serve the internal trigger positively — if the internal trigger is anxiety and the product relieves it temporarily but worsens it long-term, the hook is a trap
└─ LEAF: when designing a hook loop, run the ethical audit: "does this make the user's life better or worse a year from now?" — if the answer is ambiguous, redesign

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a product has low retention → APPLY the Hook audit: which phase of the loop is broken? Most often it's trigger-internalization or variable-reward.
- WHEN a product is hard to use → APPLY the Fogg analysis: reduce friction in the action phase before trying to increase motivation.
- WHEN a product feels boring → APPLY the variability scan: add unpredictability to the reward (content variety, surprise features, social dynamics).
- WHEN users don't return after the first try → APPLY the investment gap: what can users put into the product on their first visit that makes them want to come back?
- WHEN designing a new feature → APPLY the hook loop: does this feature complete a full Trigger → Action → Reward → Investment cycle?

## SEEDS — injectable one-liners
- "Habit forms when an internal trigger meets the fastest possible relief."
- "Don't increase motivation. Decrease friction."
- "Variable reward is not manipulation — it's how the brain works. Use it honestly or lose attention."
- "The best retention strategy: make the user invest something that makes the product worse to leave."
- "If the internal trigger is a negative emotion and your product soothes it, you own that emotion."
