# V38 · COMPETING AGAINST LUCK — Clayton Christensen
Tier 3 · Innovation Theory · Engine: Jobs-to-be-Done · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
Customers don't buy products or experiences — they HIRE them to make progress on a JOB they're trying to get done in their lives, and the product that gets hired is the one that resolves the FUNCTIONAL, SOCIAL, and EMOTIONAL dimensions of that job better than the alternatives; companies that compete on demographics or features are competing against luck.

## TRUNK — core thesis
The Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) framework: every purchase is a 'hiring' decision — the customer hires a product to do a job. The job has three dimensions: FUNCTIONAL (the practical task), SOCIAL (how they're perceived), and EMOTIONAL (how they feel). The key insight is that most companies compete on FUNCTIONAL features while the customer actually fired the old solution because of FRUSTRATIONS on the EMOTIONAL or SOCIAL dimensions — and the competitor that wins is the one that resolves the FULL job. The 'Jobs-to-be-Done' interview technique reverses-engineers the hiring decision: what pushed the customer to look? What pulled them to the new solution? What anxieties did they have? What context triggered the search? This replaces the 'who buys our product' (demographic) question with 'WHAT JOB DOES OUR PRODUCT DO?' — and most startups discover the job their product DOES is not the job they think.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The job: functional, social, emotional
├─ Functional: what the customer is trying to accomplish objectively (grind coffee, file taxes, hire a developer)
├─ Social: how they want to be seen by others (the 'thoughtful gift-giver', the 'smart investor')
├─ Emotional: how they want to feel (secure, proud, relieved, in control)
└─ LEAF: if the product only addresses the functional job, it is a commodity competing on features; the emotional and social dimensions are where pricing power lives

### Branch 2 — The 'hire/fire' moment
├─ The customer 'fires' the old solution due to a trigger — not a dissatisfaction survey answer but a real moment of frustration
├─ The 'hiring' moment has a context: a specific time, place, and situation — 'I was standing in the kitchen at 6pm on a Tuesday and realized I have nothing for dinner'
├─ The forces of progress: PUSH (problem with current), PULL (attraction of new), ANXIETY (fear of switching), HABIT (inertia of current)
└─ LEAF: reconstruct the exact hire/fire moment for 10 customers; patterns in the context reveal the real job — not the demographic

### Branch 3 — The 'must-have' test: what job is your product the ONLY solution for?
├─ A product is 'must-have' when it's the ONLY thing that does the functional + social + emotional job in a specific context
├─ If a substitute exists and feels 'good enough', the job isn't being hired for your product
├─ The Milkshake example: a milkshake competes with a banana, a bagel, and a Snickers — not other milkshakes — for the morning commute job
└─ LEAF: list the customer's alternatives — not competitors, but the OTHER things they could hire for the same job; if your product isn't the best at the full job, it's not a must-have

### Branch 4 — The JTBD interview: how to find the job
├─ No: 'what do you think of our product?' — yes: 'tell me about the last time you decided to [buy/cancel/switch]'
├─ Follow the timeline backward: what triggered the search? What did they consider? What nearly stopped them? Who influenced them?
├─ Look for the 'struggling moment' — the specific interaction with the old solution that broke the camel's back
└─ LEAF: the richest data is the SWITCHING moment; every customer has a story about why they finally left the old solution — that story IS the job

### Branch 5 — How incumbents miss the job
├─ Incumbents optimize for their existing job definition; they see the market through their product's lens
├─ The innovator's advantage is seeing a different (or more complete) job — and building the whole solution for it
├─ 'Disruption' happens when the incumbent's job definition is narrower than the job customers actually need done
└─ LEAF: the startup's best question is 'what job are incumbents NOT doing that customers are forced to solve with workarounds?' — that's the disruption opportunity

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder defines customers by demographics → APPLY job mapping: stop saying 'millennial women 25-40'; start saying 'what are they trying to get done that our product is hired for?'
- WHEN features are being compared on price → APPLY job completeness: which dimension of the job (social/emotional) is underserved? Add that — not more features
- WHEN churn is high → APPLY job-switching interview: what job were they hiring for, and did the product stop doing it? Or was the full job never solved?
- WHEN the product feels 'nice-to-have' → APPLY context reconstruction: in what specific situation is this product the ONLY reasonable hire? If none exists, the product serves a preference, not a job
- WHEN a new feature is proposed → APPLY job test: does this feature help the customer do a dimension of the job better, or does it serve our competitive benchmarking? If the latter, skip
