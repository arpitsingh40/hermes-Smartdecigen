# V37 · THE MOM TEST — Rob Fitzpatrick
Tier 3 · Customer Research · Engine: Customer Discovery · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
Most customer conversations are useless because people lie to be nice — and founders hear what they want to hear — but the problem is fixable by asking BAD QUESTIONS that force truthful answers rather than GOOD QUESTIONS that flatter; the Mom Test is a conversational discipline that prevents false positives in customer discovery.

## TRUNK — core thesis
The Mom Test rule: ask questions that would embarrass you if your mom heard you asking them — not because they're rude but because they're blunt about her behavior. Three types of bad data to avoid: COMPLIMENTS ('that's a great idea'), FLUFF (future-tense plans — 'I would totally use that'), and APPEAL TO AUTHORITY (name-dropping — 'HDFC has a team looking at this'). The solution is to ask about the CUSTOMER's LIFE AND BEHAVIOR in the past (not their opinion about your idea), anchored to specific instances. Plan to ASK THEM FOR COMMITMENT (introduction to a decision-maker, payment, timeline) by the end of the conversation — if they won't commit, they don't believe in the problem. Framing matters: 'we're trying to understand [problem area]' not 'we're building [product]'. The goal of a meeting is not to get a yes; it's to collect ZOOMED-IN truth about their actual struggles.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The three types of bad data
├─ Compliments: 'great idea, you're so smart' — pleasant, deadly, systematically misleading
├─ Fluff: hypothetical future behavior — 'I'd definitely subscribe' costs nothing to say, predicts nothing
├─ Appeal to authority: 'my VP is looking into that space' — the speaker names a third party they can't commit
└─ LEAF: the only data that matters is past behavior and concrete commitments; strip compliments from notes before they influence decisions

### Branch 2 — How to ask truthful questions
├─ Ask about their actual life: 'tell me about the last time you had this problem' — anchor to a specific instance
├─ Ask about money: 'what are you currently spending on solving this?' (even approximate)
├─ Ask about time: 'how much time did you spend on that last week?'
├─ Ask about alternatives: 'what did you do the last time [problem] happened?'
└─ LEAF: if the question can be answered with an opinion, it's bad; if it can only be answered with a specific memory, it's good

### Branch 3 — Commitment: the true signal
├─ At the end of a good conversation, ask for a real commitment: an introduction, a paid trial, a specific follow-up with a timeline
├─ 'Who else should I talk to?' + 'Can you make the intro?' — the willingness to make an intro is the strongest signal
├─ If they say 'come back when you have a product', you haven't validated the problem — you've validated their politeness
└─ LEAF: every conversation ends with a commitment ask; no commitment = the conversation was fluff, even if it felt great

### Branch 4 — The 'three meetings' test
├─ The first meeting: learn about the problem (no product mention until forced)
├─ The second meeting: show deeper understanding of their specific context
├─ The third meeting: if they're still engaged, share the solution — by now they've already co-invested in the problem
└─ LEAF: if you show the solution in meeting 1, you've ruined the discovery — now they talk about your features, not their problems

### Branch 5 — Power to the founder: running your own discovery
├─ Founders should do the first 50-100 customer conversations themselves; delegating to non-founders loses the raw signal
├─ The founder's job is not to talk about the product but to learn the customer's language, priorities, and decision process
├─ Record every conversation (with permission) — the note-taking form: facts only, no interpretations
└─ LEAF: the founder who says 'I know the customer already' has the Mom Test failure nailed — institutionalized 'I already know' is the death of discovery

### Branch 6 — The biggest failure mode: 'we validated!'
├─ 'We validated' usually means 'we got compliments' — the startup can burn 6 months on false positives
├─ The Mom Test is a counter-measure to the founder's innate optimism and the customer's innate politeness
└─ LEAF: after every customer conversation, write two columns: 'facts' (specific past behaviors observed) and 'opinions' (nice things they said) — if column 2 is longer than column 1, the test wasn't passed

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder says 'customers love it' → APPLY Mom Test audit: what specific past behaviors did they cite? If none, the 'love' is opinion, not signal
- WHEN customer discovery conversations feel good → APPLY fluff check: count the compliments; if any, the conversation failed the test
- WHEN a founder avoids asking for commitment → APPLY commitment rule: enforce the intro/timeline ask before the conversation ends
- WHEN funding decision hangs on customer feedback → APPLY past-behavior filter: strip every compliment from the data before presenting to investors
- WHEN the founder delegates discovery to junior team → APPLY founder-first: the first 50 conversations are the founder's job — no delegation
