# V71 · THE INNOVATOR'S SOLUTION — Clayton Christensen
Tier 5 · Product Innovation · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The reason established companies fail at disruption is not incompetence — it's that their processes, customers, and business models are optimized for sustaining innovation, while disruptive innovations require a completely different organizational structure, economic model, and customer focus that incumbents literally cannot adopt without abandoning their existing profit.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Christensen answers the question that The Innovator's Dilemma left open: how DO you build a company that succeeds at disruption? The answer lies in four theories: (1) the JOBS TO BE DONE framework — customers don't buy products, they hire them to get a job done; (2) the DISRUPTIVE INNOVATION theory — enter at the low end or create a new market that incumbents ignore, then improve faster than customers can adapt; (3) the INTEGRATE VS. OUTSOURCE decision — when performance is insufficient (the product isn't good enough for customers), integrate vertically to squeeze out improvement; when performance is more than enough, modularize and outsource; and (4) the BUSINESS MODEL REQUIREMENTS — a disruptive innovation needs a business model designed for the disruption, not retrofitted into the incumbent's model. The core insight: disruption is not random — it follows predictable patterns that founders can use to design their company for the disruption they intend to cause.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — Jobs to be done
├─ Customers hire products to accomplish a functional, social, or emotional job; the job is stable, the solution changes
├─ Most companies segment by demographics or product features — both miss the job the customer is actually hiring for
├─ The job includes the "hiring criteria" — the functional, social, and emotional dimensions all matter
└─ LEAF: when defining a product, force the team to write the "job statement" — "when [situation], I want to [accomplish] so that [outcome]" — before any feature discussion

### Branch 2 — The disruption pattern
├─ Disruption begins in markets that incumbents ignore: either non-consumption (new-market disruption) or low-end overserved customers
├─ The disruptor improves relentlessly; incumbents flee upward to higher margins, leaving the lower ground to the disruptor
├─ The disruptor's advantage: they can enter with a simpler, cheaper, good-enough solution that incumbents cannot compete with without destroying their own margins
└─ LEAF: when evaluating a startup's competitive position, map the incumbent's margin structure — if the startup's offer cannibalizes high-margin business, the incumbent will fight; if it targets what they're happy to lose, disruption is possible

### Branch 3 — The integrate/outsource pendulum
├─ When the product is NOT good enough (performance shortfall), integration wins — you need to control the components to improve the whole
├─ When the product is MORE than good enough (overserved), modularity wins — the market standardizes interfaces and price competition takes over
├─ The wave: in every industry, the pendulum swings from integrated to modular as performance overshoots what customers can use
└─ LEAF: when analyzing a market, ask: "are customers overserved or underserved on performance?" — integration vs. modularity strategy flows from the answer

### Branch 4 — The business model as a disruptor
├─ A disruptive innovation requires a business model that makes money at the low prices the disruption demands
├─ Incumbents cannot adopt the disruptor's business model without destroying their own — the cost structures are incompatible
├─ The solution for the founder: build the business model for the disruption from day one, not as a modification of an existing model
└─ LEAF: when a founder models unit economics, test them at the price point the incumbent CANNOT match — if they can't make money there, the model has disruption potential

### Branch 5 — The innovator's DNA: modular vs. integrated strategy
├─ The type of innovation (sustaining, low-end disruptive, new-market disruptive) determines the organizational structure needed
├─ Sustaining innovation fits inside the existing organization; disruptive innovation needs a separate unit with its own processes, values, and P&L
├─ The most common failure: trying to incubate a disruption within the core business that it will eventually cannibalize
└─ LEAF: when a founder is pursuing a potentially disruptive innovation, ask: "does this live inside the core business or in a separate unit?" — if the answer is ambiguous, the disruption will likely fail

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a product is being defined → APPLY the Jobs to Be Done framework: write the job statement before the feature list.
- WHEN a competitor is entrenched → APPLY the disruption map: what market are they happy to lose? Enter there.
- WHEN a founder debates build vs. buy → APPLY the pendulum test: is the market overserved or underserved? The answer drives the architecture.
- WHEN a new initiative conflicts with the core business → APPLY the separate-unit test: disruptive ventures need their own P&L, processes, and values.
- WHEN unit economics look tight → APPLY the disruption-price test: can this make money at a price the incumbent can't match?
