# V120 · OBVIOUSLY AWESOME — April Dunford
Tier 3 · Marketing · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
Most B2B products fail not because they're bad — but because the market doesn't understand WHY they're different and WHY that difference matters. Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how you are the best in the world at delivering value that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about. It is not messaging, not branding, not a tagline. It is the FOUNDATION on which all of those are built. Bad positioning makes great products look mediocre; great positioning makes good products look indispensable.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Dunford's positioning process has 5 components: (1) Competitive Alternatives — what would customers use if you didn't exist? Not just direct competitors, but "do nothing," spreadsheets, consultants, internal builds. (2) Unique Attributes — what features/capabilities do you have that alternatives don't? List everything, including things you take for granted. (3) Value — for each unique attribute, what specific value does it deliver? "We process 2x faster" → "You launch 3 months sooner and capture the back-to-school market." (4) Target Market Characteristics — who cares MOST about that value? The segment where your unique value is 10x, not 10% better. (5) Market Category — what existing category does the target market already understand that you can frame yourself within? "We're the CRM for construction companies." Once positioned, EVERYTHING — messaging, pricing, sales pitch, product roadmap — flows from positioning.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The 5-component positioning exercise
├─ Step 1: Competitive Alternatives — list everything a customer might do instead of using your product. "Do nothing" or "manual process" IS a competitor. Excel IS a competitor.
├─ Step 2: Unique Attributes — list every feature/capability you have that alternatives don't. Not just product features: your methodology, team expertise, business model, partnerships.
├─ Step 3: Value — link each unique attribute to specific customer value. "We have 24/7 support" → "When your factory breaks at 2am, we answer the phone and dispatch a tech within 1 hour, saving $50K/hour in downtime."
├─ Step 4: Target Market — who cares ENOUGH about this value to switch? The segment should be specific (an industry, role, company size, problem) and growing.
├─ Step 5: Market Category — what already-known category frames you? "We're ChatGPT for legal documents" vs "We're an AI-powered legal document drafting and review platform" — the first is understood in 1 second.
└─ LEAF: if you can't name at least 5 competitive alternatives (including do-nothing), you haven't thought deeply enough about why someone switches

### Branch 2 — Category design vs category adoption
├─ When to create a new category: when your product is truly different AND the existing category actively misleads the target market about what you do. This is rare and expensive.
├─ When to adopt an existing category: when your product fits an understood category but is BETTER in specific ways. "We're the CRM for construction" — CRM is known, construction specificity is the hook.
├─ New category = education burden on you. You have to teach the market what the category is before you can sell your product.
├─ Category adoption = ride existing demand while differentiating. Most startups should adopt, not create.
└─ LEAF: new categories are for companies with $50M+ in funding and 3+ years of runway to educate the market; everyone else should adopt and differentiate

### Branch 3 — Positioning before messaging
├─ Most founders skip positioning and jump directly to messaging — which is why their messaging is generic and ineffective
├─ Positioning answers: who is it for, what does it do, why is it different, why does that difference matter?
├─ Messaging translates positioning into words the target market cares about at each stage of the buyer journey
├─ Sales narrative: once positioning is clear, every rep tells the same story — "you know [CATEGORY]? We're the version for [TARGET], because we're the only one that [UNIQUE VALUE]."
└─ LEAF: if your messaging changes every time a new marketer joins, you don't have positioning — you have a revolving door of guesses

### Branch 4 — Repositioning: when what you have isn't working
├─ Signs you need repositioning: long sales cycles (buyer is confused), high churn (wrong expectations set), discount pressure (you're seen as a commodity), low win rate against one competitor (they've positioned better)
├─ Repositioning isn't rebranding — it's redefining who you're best for and why
├─ Sometimes the best repositioning is NARROWING: focus on the segment where you're 10x better, not 10% better, and own that segment before expanding
└─ LEAF: "we work for everyone" is not positioning; it's the absence of positioning

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder says "people don't get what we do" → APPLY the 5-step positioning exercise: start with competitive alternatives, work through to market category.
- WHEN messaging feels generic → APPLY the positioning-before-messaging rule: messaging is downstream; fix positioning first.
- WHEN competing against a bigger player → APPLY narrowing: what segment are you 10x better for? Be the best at that one thing.
- WHEN sales cycles are too long → APPLY the confusion check: is the buyer unsure what category you're in? Reposition into a known category with a clear differentiator.
- WHEN considering a new market → APPLY the category design test: can you afford the education cost of a new category, or should you adopt an existing one?
