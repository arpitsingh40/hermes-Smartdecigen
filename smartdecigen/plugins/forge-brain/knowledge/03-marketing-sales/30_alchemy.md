# V30 · ALCHEMY — Rory Sutherland
Tier 3 · Behavioral Economics · Engine: Creative Marketing · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The rational explanation for human behavior is almost always a post-hoc fiction; the real drivers are subconscious, irrational, and profoundly influenced by CONTEXT, PRESENTATION, and PERCEPTION — and the greatest marketing ROI comes from exploiting these irrationalities, not engineering them away.

## TRUNK — core thesis
The obsession with logic and data in business is a 'physics envy' that blinds us to the actual mechanisms of human decision-making. Sutherland argues that seemingly irrational behaviors follow a logic of their own — signalling, reciprocity, loss aversion, the endowment effect — and the most valuable business moves are often the ones that conventional MBAs would call 'stupid': the free hotel upgrade, the absurdly expensive tea, the unnecessary change that makes the experience memorable. The job of marketing is not to describe objective reality but to FRAME it — and a frame shift can be worth more than a product improvement. 'A problem that can be solved with £50m of engineering or £500 of creative thinking is not a £50m problem — it's a £500 problem that the £50m people haven't given up on yet.'

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The power of context over substance
├─ People pay more for the same coffee in a ceramic cup vs paper — the frame IS the product for the experience economy
├─ Context and presentation routinely overwhelm objective value in purchase decisions
└─ LEAF: before the founder spends on product features, audit the framing: does the price, packaging, and positioning signal the value more powerfully than the thing itself?

### Branch 2 — Signalling theory: why we buy what we buy
├─ Much consumption is signalling: the watch, the car, the hotel — not utility but identity and status signalling to others
├─ Conspicuous waste (paying more than 'necessary') IS the signal; cheapening the purchase kills its signalling value
└─ LEAF: for premium products, the 'unnecessary' premium is the product; stripping it to essential utility erodes the reason to buy

### Branch 3 — The endowment effect and loss aversion
├─ People value what they already have 2-3x more than the same thing they don't (endowment) and feel losses twice as intensely as equivalent gains (loss aversion)
├─ 'Try before you buy' and free trials work because the product becomes the customer's before the money changes hands
└─ LEAF: design every onboarding to create endowment: the user should feel ownership before payment — then loss aversion closes the sale

### Branch 4 — The placebo effect in products
├─ Expensive painkillers work better than cheap ones — even when chemically identical. Price IS part of the efficacy
├─ The placebo effect works for marketing too: the perception of quality IS quality for the user's experience
└─ LEAF: raising price can paradoxically raise satisfaction and completion rates — 'too cheap' signals 'worthless'

### Branch 5 — Why logical marketing fails
├─ The rational argument rarely converts: listing features assumes the buyer is a computer maximizing utility
├─ People decide emotionally and rationalize post-hoc; marketing that fights the post-hoc rationalization rather than the emotion is fighting the wrong fight
└─ LEAF: when the founder insists on a 'features and benefits' deck, redirect to the emotional sequence: what do they feel before → during → after using the product

### Branch 6 — Practical alchemy: cheap fixes that compound
├─ Small creative changes — rephrasing, reordering, repackaging — often produce outsized effects because they change the frame, not the product
├─ 'Ugly' but memorable beats 'pretty' but forgettable; a distinctive flaw is remembered longer than a generic perfection
└─ LEAF: institute a '£500 fix' challenge: before funding a £50k feature, the team must propose a £500 framing change and prove it won't work

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN founder insists customers are rational → APPLY post-hoc fallacy: show the gap between what people say and what they do; then reframe the purchase as a feeling-first decision
- WHEN price is the main complaint → APPLY framing audit: is the context, packaging, and category signal aligned with the value? Or is the product selling against its own presentation?
- WHEN conversion is high but repeat is low → APPLY endowment gap: the trial didn't create sufficient ownership; add a personalization step or usage milestone before payment
- WHEN a premium product is struggling → APPLY placebo price test: would raising the price AND spending the extra margin on experience improve perception and conversion?
- WHEN the brief says 'more features' → APPLY alchemy check: is the real leverage in the feature or the frame? Run the £500 fix challenge before the £50k build
