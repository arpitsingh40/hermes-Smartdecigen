# V121 · THE FOUNDER'S DILEMMAS — Noam Wasserman
Tier 3 · Leadership · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The single biggest predictor of startup failure is founder conflict — not market, product, or execution. Wasserman's 10-year study of 10,000+ founders reveals that the decisions that feel smallest at the start (who gets what equity, who gets what title, who sits on the board, who makes decisions) are the ones that destroy companies years later. Every founder faces two fundamental trade-offs: RICH vs KING (do you want to own a big slice of a small pie, or a small slice of a big pie?) and the cascading dilemmas around co-founders, hires, investors, and succession.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Founder dilemmas fall into three phases: (1) Founding Dilemmas — solo vs team, who joins, equity splits, roles/titles, relationships (friends, family, former colleagues). (2) Early-Hire Dilemmas — when to hire, who to hire (stars vs generalists), compensation (salary + equity), and the transition from founders-do-everything to specialized hires. (3) Scaling Dilemmas — when to replace yourself as CEO, how to build a board, investor dynamics, and the succession decision. In each phase, founders underestimate how much their early choices constrain their late-stage options. The most common regret: splitting equity equally when contributions were unequal.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — Rich vs King
├─ Rich: maximize the financial value of your equity — happy to give up control if it increases the pie. Accept dilution, professional CEO, outside investors.
├─ King: maximize control and autonomy — would rather own 51% of a $20M company than 5% of a $1B company.
├─ Most founders say "Rich" initially; their behavior shows "King." The gap between stated and revealed preference is the source of most investor-founder conflict.
├─ Neither is wrong — but not knowing which you are IS the mistake. Being a King who takes VC money is a marriage destined for divorce.
└─ LEAF: before you take any outside money, answer honestly: would you rather be rich or king? Then structure your funding to match.

### Branch 2 — Founding team dilemmas
├─ Solo founder vs team: teams have 2.5x higher odds of success but introduce co-founder conflict risk. The worst reason to add a co-founder: "I'm lonely" or "investors expect it."
├─ Equity splits: equal splits (50/50, 33/33/33) are the most common — and the most regretted. When contributions diverge, equal equity creates resentment.
├─ Dynamic equity: tie vesting and equity to future contributions, not past promises. 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff is standard — for founders too.
├─ The 3 R's of co-founder selection: Relationships (do you know how they handle stress?), Roles (who decides what?), Rewards (does the equity split reflect contribution?).
└─ LEAF: the best co-founder split includes a pre-nup: what happens if someone leaves in year 1, gets sick, stops contributing, or disagrees fundamentally

### Branch 3 — The CEO succession dilemma
├─ 50%+ of founders are replaced as CEO by the time the company raises a Series C. Often this is the right decision — different stages need different skills.
├─ Founder-CEO advantage: deep product knowledge, passion, vision that no hired CEO can match. Disadvantage: often can't scale with the organization (management, board dynamics, delegation).
├─ When to step aside: if you're the bottleneck, if the board has lost confidence, if you dread the CEO job, if a better operator is available and willing.
├─ How to stay: build a strong COO who complements your weaknesses, develop board management skills, actively evolve from founder to CEO — it's a learnable skill.
└─ LEAF: the transition from founder to CEO is the hardest promotion in business because nobody trains you for it AND nobody tells you it's time to go

### Branch 4 — Investor and board dynamics
├─ The first outside director changes everything — the board is no longer just the founders
├─ Board composition is the silent killer: an investor-heavy board replaces founders faster than a balanced board
├─ Lead investor relationship: this is a marriage that lasts 7-10 years. Pick the partner, not the firm. Meet the partner who'll actually sit on your board.
├─ Information asymmetry: never surprise the board. Bad news travels fast, and the board hearing it from someone other than you destroys trust permanently.
└─ LEAF: the best time to build a board relationship is before you need money; the worst is during a crisis

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder is choosing a co-founder → APPLY the 3 R's check: is the relationship tested under stress? Are roles and decision rights clear? Is the equity split fair for future contributions?
- WHEN equity is being split → APPLY the equal-split warning: equal splits are the #1 regret. Consider dynamic vesting.
- WHEN a founder faces a CEO transition → APPLY the Rich vs King framing: what kind of founder are you? What outcome do you actually want?
- WHEN conflicts erupt with co-founders → APPLY the escalation framework: have an honest conversation, not a legal one. Then mediate. Then litigate — in that order.
- WHEN considering an investor → APPLY the marriage lens: you'll work with this board member for a decade. Judge the person, not the fund's brand.
