# V119 · RADICAL CANDOR — Kim Scott
Tier 3 · Leadership · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The best bosses care personally AND challenge directly. This is Radical Candor. Most managers fall into one of three traps: Ruinous Empathy (care personally but don't challenge — praise in public, say nothing in private, person fails and it's a surprise), Obnoxious Aggression (challenge directly but don't care — brutal honesty that breaks relationships), or Manipulative Insincerity (neither care nor challenge — political backstabbing). Radical Candor is the quadrant where you care deeply enough to tell the hard truth — because their growth matters more than their comfort.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Radical Candor operates on two axes: Care Personally (giving a damn about the human being, not just the employee) and Challenge Directly (telling them when their work isn't good enough, with specificity, immediately). The framework applies to praise and criticism: praise should be specific and sincere (not "good job"), criticism should be immediate and about behavior, not character. Get giving, soliciting, and gauging guidance right: SOLICIT criticism first (show you can take it before you give it), GIVE praise and criticism often, not in annual reviews, and GAUGE how your guidance lands (ask "is there anything I could do differently to be more helpful?"). Career conversations matter: understand what each person wants (growth trajectory = steep growth vs gradual growth), not what you assume they want.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The 2x2: Care Personally × Challenge Directly
├─ Radical Candor (both high): "Your presentation missed the mark. Here are 3 specific things that didn't work and why. I know you can do better because I've seen you do it."
├─ Ruinous Empathy (care high, challenge low): "It's fine, we'll fix it together" — person doesn't know they're failing until they're fired
├─ Obnoxious Aggression (challenge high, care low): "That was terrible" — no context, no support, breaks trust and kills psychological safety
├─ Manipulative Insincerity (both low): talking behind someone's back, giving false praise to their face, political maneuvering
└─ LEAF: most founders default to Ruinous Empathy because they're nice people who hate conflict; the result is the opposite of nice

### Branch 2 — Getting guidance right
├─ Solicit before you give: ask "what could I do differently to support you better?" — go first, show you can take it
├─ Praise in public, criticize in private — BUT praise must be specific (the behavior, the impact, the result) or it's meaningless
├─ Criticism: say it immediately, say it privately, say it about the behavior/work NOT the person, say it with the assumption they can improve
├─ Don't save up criticism for performance reviews — that's a betrayal. The review should have NO surprises.
├─ "Is there anything I could do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?" — ask this every quarter
└─ LEAF: if someone is surprised by their performance review, you've failed as a manager for the previous 11 months

### Branch 3 — Growth management: superstars and rock stars
├─ Superstars (steep growth trajectory): want to be challenged, promoted, given bigger roles rapidly — give them stretch assignments, rotate them, or they'll leave
├─ Rock stars (gradual growth trajectory): happy in their role, master it, stable force — give them recognition, autonomy, and don't force them to climb
├─ Both are equally valuable. The mistake is treating rock stars as low performers or pushing superstars through roles too fast.
├─ Career conversations: "Where do you see yourself in 5 years? What skills do you want to develop? What kind of impact do you want to have?"
└─ LEAF: don't build a career plan FOR someone — ask them what they want, then help them get there. The best retention strategy is making someone's current job the best path to their dream job

### Branch 4 — Firing and team management
├─ If you've given honest feedback consistently (no surprises), the person usually knows when it's time. The firing conversation should be sad but not shocking.
├─ Firing should be for performance (behavior/work output), not personality, with documented feedback trail
├─ After a firing: explain to the team what happened (with the person's permission), don't let rumor fill the vacuum
├─ Meetings: staff meetings (debate decisions, not updates), 1:1s (employee's agenda, not yours), think time (blocked, sacred), all-hands (context, celebration)
└─ LEAF: 1:1s are not status updates — status can be a 2-line email. 1:1s are for coaching, career talk, and the stuff they'd never put in writing

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder is avoiding a hard conversation → APPLY the Ruinous Empathy check: what's the cost of NOT saying this? Is the short-term comfort worth the long-term damage?
- WHEN giving feedback → APPLY the CPR framework: Content (the specific work, once), Pattern (if it repeats, it's a pattern), Relationship (if it still repeats, it's a trust issue).
- WHEN a top performer might leave → APPLY the superstar vs rock star diagnosis: what do they actually want from their career? Are you giving it to them?
- WHEN a performance review is coming up → APPLY the no-surprises rule: have you given this feedback already, in the moment, every time?
- WHEN a team member is struggling → APPLY Care Personally: understand their context first. Then Challenge Directly: name the specific gap, offer support.
