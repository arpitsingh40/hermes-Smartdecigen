# V50 · THE HALO EFFECT — Phil Rosenzweig
Tier 3 · Business Criticism · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
Almost every claim about what makes companies successful is TAUTOLOGICAL — performance halo (the glow of success) colors every attribute we measure about a company, so "successful companies have great strategy/culture/leadership" is usually just "we think great companies have what great companies have."

## TRUNK — core thesis
Rosenzweig systematically dismantles the bestselling business books (Built to Last, Good to Great, In Search of Excellence) by showing that their research methods suffer from the HALO EFFECT: when a company is financially successful, observers rate all its attributes positively (strategy, culture, leadership, values); when the same company declines, those same attributes get rated negatively. The "data" in these studies — survey responses, executive interviews, case studies — is contaminated by halo. Correlations between attributes and performance may be artifacts, not causes. Further, many successful companies regress to the mean, but the books attribute the decline to a failure of the very practices they claimed caused the success. Rosenzweig's positive contribution: performance is driven by STRATEGY (positioning and choices) plus EXECUTION (operational effectiveness) — not by culture or values magic — and the only honest way to study it is through careful causal identification.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The halo mechanism
├─ When a company is performing well, everything looks good: strategy is visionary, culture is strong, leadership is brilliant, values are authentic
├─ When the same company performs poorly: strategy was flawed, culture was weak, leadership failed, values were hollow
├─ The actual attributes didn't change that much — the performance colored the perception
└─ LEAF: for any claimed "driver of success," ask: was this measurement independent of the company's financial performance at the time? If the rater knew the performance, the measure is likely halo-contaminated

### Branch 2 — Methodological sins in business books
├─ Selecting on the dependent variable: studying only successful companies and inferring what made them successful (no comparison group of failures)
├─ Confusing correlation with causation: successful companies have strong cultures; does culture cause success, or does success create the perception of strong culture?
├─ The narrative fallacy: constructing a coherent story after the fact ("they succeeded because of X, Y, Z") — the story satisfies but may have nothing to do with causation
├─ Regression to the mean: extreme performance is followed by more average performance — books attribute the decline to a change in the company's practices when it's just statistical
└─ LEAF: when a founder cites a business book's formula for success, the engine's first response: "did that study control for halo? Did it have a control group? Was performance measured independently of the attributes?"

### Branch 3 — The specific critique of Good to Great (V42)
├─ Collins' Level 5 leaders were identified AFTER the companies became great — the halo meant researchers saw humility+will because of performance, not as a cause
├─ The Hedgehog Concept correlating with success: did the concept precede success, or was the concept reinterpreted after success to fit the narrative?
├─ The companies that "fell from greatness" (Fannie Mae, Circuit City) show that the formula didn't protect them — regression to the mean and strategic shocks overwhelmed any "greatness" factor
└─ LEAF: Good to Great is a useful HYPOTHESIS generator, but its claims should be treated as testable propositions, not established facts; test the Level 5 or Hedgehog assertions on the founder's own situation with independent measures

### Branch 4 — What actually drives performance
├─ Strategy: industry structure (Porter's five forces) and competitive position matter enormously — this is measurable and partially independent
├─ Execution: operational effectiveness, cost control, quality, speed — also measurable
├─ Luck: a substantial portion of variance in company performance is unexplained by any controllable factor
└─ LEAF: for a given founder, the honest causal model is: performance = strategy choices × execution quality × luck; culture/values/leadership matter only insofar as they affect strategy and execution — and their true effect is much smaller than the halo literature claims

### Branch 5 — Implications for learning from success stories
├─ Reading success stories is MOTIVATING but not INSTRUCTIVE — the stories are biased by halo, selection, and narrative construction
├─ Better learning source: studying FAILURES (they have less halo: nobody glorifies a failed company's culture) and studying COMPARISONS (similar companies with different outcomes)
├─ The most honest data: careful within-industry comparisons, independent of outcome, with controls for known confounders
└─ LEAF: when a founder says "let's do what Company X did," the engine should ask: do we know that Company X's practices PRECEDED its success? Are we comparing against their failures? Or are we buying a halo story?

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder cites a success story as a model → APPLY halo skepticism: was the cited attribute measured before or after success? Is there a control group of failures with the same attribute?
- WHEN evaluating a company's own performance → APPLY halo debias: separate the narrative of "what we do well" from the independent performance metrics — the narrative is likely halo-inflated in good times and halo-deflated in bad.
- WHEN a founder over-attributes their success to culture/values → APPLY strategy + execution grounding: how much of the result is explained by industry positioning and operational choices? Name the honesty gap.
- WHEN looking for strategic lessons → APPLY failure study: identify a comparable failure and compare; the contrast is more instructive than the success arc.
- WHEN performance declines after a period of success → APPLY regression to mean: before concluding that strategy/culture broke, check if the earlier success was an extreme that was due for correction.

## SEEDS — injectable one-liners
- "A company succeeds → everything looks good. That's the halo. Cause or perception?"
- "If the attribute was measured AFTER the result, it's not a cause — it's a description."
- "Read success stories for motivation. Study failures for instruction."
- "Performance = strategy × execution × luck. The first two you control; the third you don't."
- "Don't ask what successful companies do. Ask what they did DIFFERENTLY from unsuccessful ones."
