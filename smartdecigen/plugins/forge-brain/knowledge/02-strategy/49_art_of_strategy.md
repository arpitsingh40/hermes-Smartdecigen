# V49 · THE ART OF STRATEGY — Avinash K. Dixit & Barry J. Nalebuff
Tier 2 · Game Theory · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
Every strategic interaction is a GAME — your move affects others' outcomes and their responses affect yours — and winning means thinking in terms of INTERDEPENDENCE: not just "what should I do?" but "what will they do in response to what I do, and what should I do knowing that?"

## TRUNK — core thesis
Game theory made accessible: strategy is the art of making moves that account for the countermoves of others. Core concepts: dominant strategies (a move that's best regardless of what others do), Nash equilibrium (each player's best response to the others' best responses), credible commitment (binding yourself to a course of action to change others' expectations), brinkmanship (pushing a conflict to the edge of disaster to force a favorable outcome), prisoner's dilemma (individual rationality leads to collective disaster unless you change the game), and co-opetition (simultaneous competition AND cooperation — creating value cooperatively, capturing it competitively). The book teaches HOW to think strategically by identifying the GAME you're in and changing its structure: adding or removing players, changing payoffs, changing the information available, or changing the timing of moves.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — Identify the game you're in
├─ Simultaneous vs sequential: are you moving together (pricing decisions) or one after another (negotiation)?
├─ Zero-sum vs non-zero-sum: is one player's gain exactly another's loss, or can both win?
├─ Repeated vs one-shot: reputation and cooperation are possible in repeated games; defection dominates one-shots
├─ Complete vs incomplete information: do you know their payoffs and options, or are you guessing?
└─ LEAF: before any strategic move, classify the game type — the optimal move differs completely by game; the most common error is treating a non-zero-sum game as zero-sum (destroying value) or a repeated game as one-shot (forgoing cooperation)

### Branch 2 — Credible commitment: the power of constraint
├─ A threat/bargain is only effective if the other party believes you'll follow through — if it's not credible, it's noise
├─ How to make a commitment credible: burn bridges (remove the option of retreat), use reputation (past behavior as collateral), write contracts, delegate to an agent with different incentives, use brinkmanship
├─ The hostage: giving the other party something they value that you'll lose if you defect — a mutual commitment device
└─ LEAF: when a founder says "we'll leave the market if this doesn't work" or "our final price is X", test credibility: what have you actually done to commit? If nothing, the other party should rationally ignore the statement

### Branch 3 — Prisoner's dilemma: changing the game
├─ In the classic PD, each player's individual rational choice leads to a collectively worse outcome
├─ For business: price wars, advertising arms races, and feature wars are PDs — individual defection is rational, collective result is destruction
├─ Solutions: change payoffs (merge, collude — but watch antitrust), change the game structure (make it repeated through industry visibility), change players' identities (build trust through shared ventures)
└─ LEAF: when a founder is in a price war or feature war, name the PD structure; the solution is not to compete harder inside the PD — it's to change the game itself (differentiate, change the metric customers compare on, or add switching costs)

### Branch 4 — Brinkmanship: controlled risk
├─ The art of pushing a conflict to the edge without falling over: a deliberate, public, partial loss of control that makes the potential disaster visible to both sides
├─ Nuclear standoff as model: each side signals "I may not be able to stop escalation even if I want to"
├─ In negotiation: walking out, setting a deadline, involving a third party, making a "final" offer that is uncomfortable to retract
└─ LEAF: brinkmanship is high-risk and works only if the opponent believes you're willing to accept the disaster; founders should only use brinkmanship when the downside of the current path is worse than the brink itself

### Branch 5 — Co-opetition: creating value together, capturing it separately
├─ Business is not purely competitive — it's simultaneous competition AND cooperation
├─ Cooperation: with suppliers (joint innovation), customers (co-creation), complementors (companies whose products make yours more valuable — software and hardware, content and platform)
├─ Competition: in the capture of the created value (pricing, terms)
├─ The Value Net: map your company, customers, suppliers, competitors, and complementors — the last (complementors) is the most overlooked
└─ LEAF: when a founder sees only competitors and customers, add the COMPLEMENTOR lens — whose success helps your success? Can you partner with them to expand the pie before dividing it?

### Branch 6 — Information games: signaling and screening
├─ Signaling: sending a costly, observable action that credibly communicates your private information (a warranty signals quality because only high-quality firms can afford it)
├─ Screening: designing a choice that sorts others by their private information (offering two pricing tiers screens customers by willingness to pay)
├─ Adverse selection: hidden information causes bad outcomes (only bad products offered at a pooled price) — solved by signaling and screening
└─ LEAF: when a founder has better information than investors/customers, they must signal it credibly (expensive signal); when they lack information, they must screen (design choices that reveal the truth)

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder faces a competitor move → APPLY game identification: classify the game type (simultaneous/sequential, zero-sum/non-zero, repeated/one-shot) — prescribe the move set accordingly.
- WHEN a threat or promise is made → APPLY credibility test: what commitment mechanism makes this believable? If none, treat it as noise.
- WHEN a price war or feature war begins → APPLY prisoner's dilemma reframe: the solution is to change the game, not compete harder within it.
- WHEN a founder negotiates → APPLY brinkmanship calibration: is this worth the edge? What's the commitment device?
- WHEN the founder only sees competitors → APPLY Value Net expansion: add complementors — whose growth helps your growth? Partner before fight.

## SEEDS — injectable one-liners
- "What game are you playing? Name it before you make a move."
- "A threat only works if the other side believes it. How have you committed?"
- "Price wars are prisoner's dilemmas — the only way to win is to change the rules."
- "Whose success helps your success? Those are complementors. Partner with them."
- "If you know something they don't, signal it expensively. If they know something you don't, screen for it."
