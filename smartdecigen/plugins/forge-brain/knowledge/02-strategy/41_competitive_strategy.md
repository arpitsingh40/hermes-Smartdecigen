# V41 · COMPETITIVE STRATEGY — Michael Porter
Tier 2 · Business Strategy · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
Competition is not about being better — it is about being DIFFERENT: a firm outperforms rivals only if it occupies a distinctive position that delivers superior value, AND builds activities that reinforce each other in a way competitors cannot cheaply replicate without sacrificing their own position.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Porter's framework is the foundational language of strategy: five forces determine industry attractiveness (rivalry, threat of entry, supplier power, buyer power, substitutes); three generic strategies are the only coherent paths to advantage (cost leadership, differentiation, focus); every position requires a TAILORED ACTIVITY SYSTEM where choices in one function amplify choices in another. The worst state is STUCK IN THE MIDDLE — no clear cost advantage and no defensible differentiation. Growth that blurs position destroys more value than it creates. Strategy is about TRADE-OFFS: serving one set of customers well necessarily means serving others less well.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — The five forces (industry structure)
├─ Threat of entry: barriers (scale, capital, switching costs, incumbency advantages independent of price) protect profit
├─ Supplier power: concentrated suppliers extract margin; fragmented suppliers concede it
├─ Buyer power: concentrated buyers extract price; fragmented buyers pay price
├─ Threat of substitutes: not just other products — anything meeting the same need at different price/performance
├─ Rivalry intensity: structural (many equal players, slow growth, high exit barriers) most determines profit
└─ LEAF: an attractive industry is not one with big revenue — it's one where these five forces combine to let the average player earn above-cost returns

### Branch 2 — The three generic strategies
├─ Cost leadership: structural cost advantage (scale, process, access) that lets you profit at prices competitors can't match — not discounting, EARNED lower cost
├─ Differentiation: uniqueness valued by customers that justifies a premium; must be real and perceived, and costly for rivals to match
├─ Focus: serve a narrow segment SUPERBLY, either by lowest cost to that segment or by best meeting its particular needs
└─ LEAF: every founder must answer "which ONE of these doors am I walking through — and which customers and activities am I deliberately excluding?"

### Branch 3 — Activities, not strategy statements
├─ Strategy lives in the configuration of daily activities that reinforce each other; a positioning statement is cheap, an aligned activity system is hard
├─ Trade-offs make strategy durable: choosing what NOT to do prevents imitation because copying the whole system means unmaking the imitator's system
├─ Fit types: simple consistency (activities reinforce the brand), reinforcing (activities amplify each other's effect), optimization of effort (no redundancy or conflict)
└─ LEAF: evaluate a strategy by the activity system it implies, not the words it uses — if the day-to-day hasn't changed, the strategy hasn't

### Branch 4 — Growth trap: blurring the position
├─ The most common destroyer: adding product lines, channels, or segments that don't fit the activity system
├─ Each addition makes trade-offs muddier, activities less reinforcing, and imitation easier
└─ LEAF: before expanding, map how the new segment/offering fits the existing activity system; if it requires new activities that conflict with existing ones, it's a trap

### Branch 5 — Stuck in the middle
├─ Neither cost leader nor differentiator nor focused: competing on price without the structure to sustain it, and on differentiation without the uniqueness to command premium
├─ Almost always the growth-by-customer-pressure path: one big customer demands lower price AND more service
└─ LEAF: when the founder can't articulate their primary generic strategy, the engine must diagnose the stuck-in-the-middle state and force the choice

### Branch 6 — Industry analysis for founders
├─ Porter was analyzing FORTUNE 500 incumbents; founders face a different question: is this industry structure ATTAINABLE? Is the five-force picture findable BEFORE the incumbent defends?
├─ Focus on ENTRY barrier structure specifically: can you build a defensible position in a niche that big players find unattractive to copy?
└─ LEAF: for a founder, the forces that matter most are barriers to entry (can I get in?) and barriers to exit (can I leave if wrong?) — study those first

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder plans growth → APPLY activity-system audit: does the new product/channel/customer fit the existing activity configuration, or does it blur the position?
- WHEN a founder faces margin pressure → APPLY five-force diagnosis: which force is extracting margin? Supplier, buyer, or rival? Target the solution at the specific force.
- WHEN a founder can't articulate their advantage → APPLY generic strategy forcing: cost leader, differentiator, focus — pick one, exclude the other two, defend the choice.
- WHEN a founder adds features to compete with everyone → APPLY stuck-in-the-middle warning: the activity system is likely conflicting — simplify to one logic.
- WHEN industry analysis is needed → APPLY barrier-first: tell the founder which two forces matter at their stage (entry and exit), skip the rest.

## SEEDS — injectable one-liners
- "Competition isn't about being better — it's about being different in activities."
- "Pick a door: cost, differentiation, or focus. Then burn the other two."
- "If everything fits, nothing fits — trade-offs make strategy real."
- "What are you deliberately NOT doing? That's your strategy."
- "An activity system is harder to copy than a product."
