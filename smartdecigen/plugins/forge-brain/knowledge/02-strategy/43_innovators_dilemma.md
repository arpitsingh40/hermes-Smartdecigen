# V43 · THE INNOVATOR'S DILEMMA — Clayton Christensen
Tier 3 · Strategy · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The most dangerous threat to an incumbent is not better technology from a competitor — it is DISRUPTIVE INNOVATION: a simpler, cheaper, lower-margin offering that the incumbent rationally ignores because it serves smaller, less profitable customers, until that offering improves enough to capture the mainstream — by which point the incumbent is trapped.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Christensen separates innovation into two kinds: SUSTAINING (better performance for existing customers — incumbents almost always win these) and DISRUPTIVE (worse performance by existing metrics but cheaper/simpler/more accessible — attackers win these). The DILEMMA: incumbents have the resources to respond but CANNOT rationally allocate them to disruptive threats because their best customers don't want the low-end product and their margin structure demands higher returns. The only escape is to set up an AUTONOMOUS UNIT that can legitimately pursue the low-end model without the incumbent's cost structure. For startups: disruption is a viable strategy only if it attacks the bottom of the market or competes against non-consumption.

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — Sustaining vs disruptive innovation
├─ Sustaining: better along the dimensions mainstream customers historically value (faster processor, higher resolution) — incumbents win because their R&D budget and customer insight advantage compound
├─ Disruptive: worse along traditional dimensions but cheaper/simpler/easier — mainstream customers reject it initially; it improves along the attackers' trajectory until it meets mainstream needs
└─ LEAF: classify every new entry: if it serves existing customers with better performance, it's sustaining (incumbent wins); if it serves overshot customers or non-consumers with simpler/cheaper, it's disruptive (attacker wins)

### Branch 2 — The innovator's dilemma mechanism
├─ Incumbent's resource allocation is driven by existing customers and margin structure
├─ Disruptive products have lower margins, serve smaller markets, and require different channel/sales approaches — the rational resource-allocation process starves them
├─ Incumbent sees the threat, KNOWS it matters, and still cannot respond because their own weight (customer base, margin expectations, organizational process) won't let them
└─ LEAF: when a big competitor dismisses a founder's idea, it's not necessarily arrogance — it's rational behavior given their economics; test whether they COULD respond without wrecking their own business

### Branch 3 — The disruptive strategy for founders
├─ Attack where the incumbent is happy to lose: low-end segments they don't serve well, or non-consumption (people who can't afford/access the existing solution)
├─ The product must be CHEAPER first; quality improves over time; if you try to enter at the same price with better features, you're playing the incumbent's game
├─ Disruption works when the incumbent OVER-SHOOTS: customers stop valuing the incremental improvements the incumbent keeps investing in
└─ LEAF: a founder's clearest disruption signal is a big competitor that barely acknowledges the segment — if they defend, it's sustaining; if they ignore, it's disruption

### Branch 4 — The autonomy imperative
├─ The only way an incumbent can respond to disruption is with a SPIN-OFF organization: independent P&L, different cost structure, different customer set, different decision rules
├─ Attempting a disruptive offering within the core business inevitably fails because core processes are optimized for the existing model
└─ LEAF: when a founder becomes the incumbent (post-PMF with growing headcount), the discipline is to NOT launch adjacent innovations internally — spin them off with their own decision rights

### Branch 5 — Jobs to Be Done (Christensen's later extension)
├─ People don't buy products; they HIRE them to do a job that arises in their life
├─ The job is functional, social, emotional — and understanding the full job reveals why customers behave seemingly irrationally
├─ Segment by job, not by demographic; the job is stable, demographics shift
└─ LEAF: when a founder describes customers by age/income/location, reframe the question to: what JOB are they hiring your product for? What's the alternative they currently hire?

### Branch 6 — Disruption against non-consumption
├─ The safest disruptive entry: people who want the capability but can't access it (too expensive, too complex, too inconvenient)
├─ You're not taking customers from anyone — you're expanding the market; incumbents have no incentive to respond
└─ LEAF: for a 0-to-1 startup, the best competitive landscape is one where the answer to "who do you take customers from?" is "no one — we serve people who couldn't do this before"

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder enters an existing market → APPLY disruption test: is the incumbent overshooting? Are you attacking from below or from non-consumption? If entering at parity, warn of sustaining-game risk.
- WHEN a founder is dismissed by an incumbent → APPLY dilemma reversal: the dismissal is a green flag if the threat is genuinely disruptive; red flag if the threat is sustaining (they'll respond and crush you).
- WHEN a founder adds features to match incumbents → APPLY trajectory check: are you climbing the disruption curve (cheaper → better) or jumping to the sustaining curve (playing their game)?
- WHEN the founder builds a product without understanding purchase behavior → APPLY JTBD reframe: what job is being hired for? What's the current hire? What's the full context?
- WHEN a founder becomes the incumbent → APPLY autonomy warning: the next innovation needs its own decision structure, not a division.

## SEEDS — injectable one-liners
- "The incumbent can't respond because responding hurts them more than ignoring you."
- "Better is not always winning — it depends which trajectory you're on."
- "Are you attacking from below or from non-consumption?"
- "What job are your customers hiring your product to do?"
- "If the biggest player ignores your segment, it's a signal. Figure out which one."
