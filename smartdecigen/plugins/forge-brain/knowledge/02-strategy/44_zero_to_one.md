# V44 · ZERO TO ONE — Peter Thiel
Tier 3 · Innovation · Tree Memory

## ROOT — the deepest truth
The greatest business is a MONOPOLY — a company that owns a large share of a small, well-defined market and builds such deep advantage that competitors can't enter; "competition is for losers" because competitive markets destroy profits by definition, while monopolies capture and compound them.

## TRUNK — core thesis
Thiel argues from first principles: technology (doing new things) moves from 0 to 1; globalization (doing existing things everywhere) moves 1 to n. Founders should pursue the monopoly route: start small (dominate a niche), scale up (expand into adjacent markets), don't disrupt (avoid incumbents' attention), and build on a SECRET — a contrarian truth most people don't believe. Power law: a single investment/effort will be worth more than all others combined — so focus on the ONE thing. The seven questions every business must answer: engineering (can you create breakthrough tech?), timing (is now the right time?), monopoly (start with a small market share?), people (do you have the right team?), distribution (can you reach customers?), durability (will your position last?), and the secret (have you identified a unique opportunity?).

## BRANCHES & LEAVES

### Branch 1 — Monopoly: the only durable business model
├─ Perfect competition destroys profits; monopolies capture them — the goal is not to compete but to escape competition entirely
├─ Monopoly characteristics: proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, brand
├─ Start with a SMALL market you can dominate (tiny share of huge market = zero power; large share of tiny market = foundation)
└─ LEAF: when a founder describes a huge addressable market, push back — "what's the smallest market you can dominate FIRST?" — monopoly is built in concentric circles from a core

### Branch 2 — Secrets: the contrarian truth
├─ Every great company is built around a SECRET — something important and true that most people don't believe
├─ If there are no secrets left, every opportunity would be exploited to equilibrium — clearly they exist
├─ The secret is often specific, local, and non-obvious even to industry insiders
└─ LEAF: ask the founder "what important truth do very few people agree with you on?" — if the answer is generic, they haven't found their secret yet

### Branch 3 — Start small and monopolize
├─ Amazon: started with books (a niche Amazon could dominate) → expanded to every category
├─ Facebook: started with Harvard → other Ivy League → all US colleges → the world
├─ LinkedIn: started with the professional networking niche for US executives, then expanded
├─ PayPal: started with the niche of Palm Pilot power users sending money between devices
└─ LEAF: a founder's inability to name the ONE niche they will dominate in the next 12 months is a sign they haven't chosen — everyone's customer = no one's

### Branch 4 — Power law thinking
├─ The venture capital power law: a single investment returns more than all others combined — this applies to founders too (one initiative beats the rest)
├─ Portfolio thinking (spreading efforts across many bets) is rational only if you can't predict which bet wins; but a startup CANNOT afford portfolio logic — it must bet everything on the single best opportunity
└─ LEAF: when a founder has multiple projects/initiatives running in parallel, apply the power law: which one initiative, if it works, makes everything else irrelevant? Double down there, kill the rest.

### Branch 5 — Distribution matters as much as product
├─ Great product × bad distribution = dead company; great distribution × average product = big company
├─ Thiel ranks distribution channels: viral (product spreads by use), paid (advertising), content (SEO/PR) — viral is ideal but rarest
├─ Sales is hidden: the best salespeople don't look like salespeople (founders selling to early customers often look like educators/consultants)
└─ LEAF: evaluate distribution upfront: how does the first 1000 customers happen? If the answer is "growth hacking" or "word of mouth" without a mechanism, it's a hope, not a plan

### Branch 6 — The seven questions framework
├─ Engineering: can you create breakthrough tech or just incremental improvement?
├─ Timing: is now the right time? Is the market ready?
├─ Monopoly: are you starting with a small share of a big market, or most of a small market?
├─ People: do you have complementary founding team chemistry?
├─ Distribution: do you have a real channel to customers?
├─ Durability: will your position last after you succeed?
├─ Secret: what unique truth are you acting on that no one else believes?
└─ LEAF: run the seven questions as a checklist on every opportunity; a "no" on any question is a critical weakness, not a minor detail

## FRUIT — how SmartDecigen applies this
- WHEN a founder pitches a huge TAM → APPLY monopoly-first: "smallest market you can dominate completely?" — define the entry niche.
- WHEN a founder has too many initiatives → APPLY power law forcing: identify the one bet that dominates, push to kill or defer everything else.
- WHEN a founder's story is generic → APPLY the secret question: what do you believe that few others agree with? No conviction = no differentiated strategy.
- WHEN a founder has a great product but no distribution plan → APPLY distribution priority: map the actual channel for the first 1000 customers; if the channel is vague, product will die waiting.
- WHEN evaluating the founding team → APPLY the chemistry check: do they complement each other's skills and share a unified vision? Conflict at the top kills before the market gets a vote.

## SEEDS — injectable one-liners
- "Competition is for losers. What monopoly are you building?"
- "What secret do you believe that almost no one agrees with?"
- "Start so small you can dominate it. Then expand."
- "Which one bet outweighs all others combined? Double down, kill the rest."
- "A great product with no distribution is a museum exhibit."
